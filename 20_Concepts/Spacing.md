# Spacing

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Spacing means reviewing on different days instead of cramming in one sitting.
> The gap that helps most grows with how long you need to remember the material.
> Cramming wins right after the session and loses weeks later, which is exactly when the exam is.

## Study explanation

Massed practice feels stronger because everything is fresh: one long session, the material still in short-term memory, and a quiz right after goes well. The problem is that the memory fades fast. Spacing trades that short-term comfort for durability: each review happens after some forgetting, and rebuilding the memory after a gap makes it stronger and longer-lasting.

The evidence comes from large-scale syntheses, not from one lab. Cepeda and colleagues (2006) meta-analyzed 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317 experiments and found that spacing beats massing overall, and that the best gap between study sessions grows as the delay to the final test grows: the interstudy interval that produces maximal retention increases as the retention interval increases ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F2]). Cepeda and colleagues (2008) then tested this over long durations with more than 1,350 people taught a set of facts, a review after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and a final test up to 1 year later. At any given test delay, increasing the gap between study sessions first improved and then reduced performance, and the optimal gap was roughly 10 to 20 percent of the test delay for short delays, shrinking to about 5 to 10 percent for a one-year delay ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F3]). In plain terms: if the exam is in a week, leave about a day between sessions; if you need the material for a year, review after weeks, not hours.

A worked example. You have a certification exam in 30 days and ten chapters to cover. The massed plan: read all ten chapters today, then quiz yourself tonight and feel done. The spaced plan: read chapters 1 and 2 today, review them tomorrow, add chapters 3 and 4 the day after, and keep cycling back over everything with growing gaps. Quiz yourself right after today's session and the massed plan looks better. Quiz yourself on day 29 and the spaced plan wins, because each review landed after real forgetting and rebuilt the memory more durably. The same pattern shows up in classrooms: Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated ten common study techniques and gave their highest utility rating to only two, practice testing and distributed practice, while cramming-style rereading rated low ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F4]).

The most common mistake is to judge the plan by how the session felt. A crammed session ends with a high quiz score and a feeling of mastery, and that feeling is exactly what misleads you: it measures short-term familiarity, not retention. The fix is to schedule the review before you feel ready, and to let the calendar, not your confidence, decide when the next session happens. A second mistake is spacing so evenly that every gap is too short for the goal: the 2008 study found the optimal gap is a fraction of the total time you need to remember, so a one-year skill needs gaps of weeks, not hours.

Spacing connects to two other ideas in this vault. Retrieval practice: each spaced review should be an active recall, not a reread, because testing and spacing are the two techniques with the strongest evidence. Desirable difficulty: the gap makes recall harder, and that effort is the point, not a side effect.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** review on different days, and let the gap grow with how long you need the material: about 10 to 20 percent of the time until the test (Cepeda et al., 2008).
- **Common confusion:** "I did well right after studying" is short-term performance; spacing is judged by the score weeks later.
- **Exercise:** take any note you studied today, schedule one review tomorrow and one next week, and write down what you forgot at each.
