# Spacing

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Reviewing on different days beats reviewing all at once, for the same total time.
> The best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember the material.
> Cramming feels stronger right after it, which is exactly why it fools you.

## Study explanation

Spacing means distributing study or review sessions over time instead of massing them into one sitting. The effect is one of the most replicated findings in memory research: the same material, the same total study time, but reviews on different days leave a stronger memory than one long session ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F2] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/).

Cepeda and colleagues (2006) reviewed 839 assessments of the distributed practice effect from 317 experiments in 184 articles [F2] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/. They examined two separate questions: spacing (separate study episodes versus one massed episode) and lag (a longer gap between episodes versus a shorter one). Both help, and they work together with the retention interval: the gap that produces maximal retention increases as the delay to the final test increases. In other words, the right gap depends on how long you need to remember.

Cepeda and colleagues (2008) tested that interaction directly [F3] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/. More than 1,350 people learned a set of facts, came back after a gap of up to 3.5 months for a review, and took a final test up to a year later. At any given test delay, increasing the gap between study sessions first improved and then gradually reduced final performance: there is an optimal gap, not "the longer the better". Measured as a share of the test delay, the optimal gap was about 20 to 40% for a one-week test delay but only 5 to 10% for a one-year delay. The authors' conclusion is blunt: the interaction implies that many common study practices are highly inefficient.

A worked example. You have a certification exam in eight weeks and 20 hours of study time. The massed plan: two 10-hour days the weekend before the exam. The spaced plan: four 5-hour sessions, one every two weeks, each ending with a self-test. Right after each session both plans feel equal. At the exam, the spaced plan wins, because the optimal gap for an eight-week retention interval is on the order of one to three weeks, and each spaced review lands near it. The massed plan puts all its reviews at a gap of zero relative to the test.

The most common mistake is to judge study by how it feels immediately after. Massed practice produces high performance in the moment, so it feels like learning; spaced practice produces lower performance right after the session, so it feels like forgetting. That feeling is backwards: the forgetting between spaced sessions is part of what makes the later retrieval strong ([[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]]). A second mistake is to space everything at the same fixed gap. Because the optimal gap grows with the retention interval, a gap that is right for tomorrow's quiz is too short for next year's exam.

Spacing connects to two other ideas in this vault. Retrieval practice: spacing only pays off when each review is an active recall attempt, not a reread. Interleaving: mixing topics inside each spaced session adds a second, smaller benefit when the topics look similar.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** spread reviews over days, and let the gap grow with how long you need to remember. For a test in one week, review after about 2 to 3 days; for a test in two months, review after about 1 to 3 weeks ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F3] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/).
- **Common confusion:** "I did better right after cramming" is performance in the moment, not retention at the test. Spaced practice looks worse immediately and wins later.
- **Exercise:** take a note you studied today. Without looking, write what you remember, then schedule the same recall for tomorrow, for three days from now, and for one week from now.

## Going deeper

The 2006 meta-analysis reports that the distributed practice effect appears across ages, materials and test types, which is why Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) gave distributed practice their top utility rating alongside practice testing ([[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], [F4]). Expanding schedules (growing gaps, e.g. 1 day, 3 days, 7 days) are a practical approximation of the optimal-gap curve, but Cepeda et al. (2008) caution that a fixed expanding schedule is not optimal for every retention interval: match the gap to the test date instead.
